Causal Inference

  • Goal: Estimate the effect of a treatment \(T\) on an outcome \(Y\).
  • Potential outcomes (Neyman, 1923; Rubin, 1974): for each unit \(i\) there are two potential outcomes:
    • \(Y_i(1)\): outcome if treated
    • \(Y_i(0)\): outcome if not treated
  • Individual treatment effect: \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\)
  • Average treatment effect: \(\text{ATE} = \text{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]\)

The Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference

We never observe both \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\) for the same unit. One potential outcome is always missing: the counterfactual.

\[ \text{Observed: } Y_i = T_i \cdot Y_i(1) + (1 - T_i) \cdot Y_i(0) \]

A naive comparison of treated and untreated units can be biased because the two groups can differ in confounders.

Bayesian Statistics

We use Bayes’ theorem to update our beliefs about parameters \(\theta\) after we observe data \(y\):

\[ \underbrace{p(\theta \mid y)}_{\text{posterior}} \propto \underbrace{p(y \mid \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}} \]

  • Prior \(p(\theta)\): domain knowledge before we see the data.
  • Likelihood \(p(y \mid \theta)\): how probable the data is for given parameters.
  • Posterior \(p(\theta \mid y)\): updated beliefs after we see the data.

From Generative Model to Inference

A probabilistic program describes how the data is generated. Before we observe data, every variable has a prior distribution. When we condition on observed data, the same program defines the likelihood. If we read its arrows as causal, the same program is both the causal model and the Bayesian model.

Probabilistic Programming Languages (PPLs)

PPLs let us write generative models as code and run posterior inference automatically. Examples: PyMC, NumPyro, Stan.

Causal Inference and PPLs

In industry, we estimate causal effects from limited data and under assumptions we cannot fully test. PPLs help in the following ways:

  • The model is the DAG: we write the data-generating process as code, so every causal assumption is explicit.

  • Priors: domain knowledge and past experiments enter the estimate.

  • Uncertainty: we get a posterior distribution for every causal quantity.

  • The do operator: we apply interventions to the model and compute counterfactual predictions (pm.do in PyMC, numpyro.handlers.do in NumPyro).

  • Flexible structure: non-linear likelihoods, hierarchical models, latent variables, and measurement models. The do operator still applies.

Every framework, Bayesian or frequentist, has assumptions and limitations. PPLs do not solve identification. They help with estimation once we state the causal assumptions.

Bayesian A/B Testing

In an A/B test, we randomly assign users to a control group and a treatment group. Random assignment makes the treatment independent of all confounders, observed and unobserved. The hard part moves from identification to estimation under uncertainty. We observe \(y_\text{c}\) and \(y_\text{t}\) conversions out of \(n\) users per group, and we add a Binomial likelihood for them later.

with pm.Model() as non_informative_model:
    conversion_rate_control = pm.Uniform(
        "conversion_rate_control", lower=0, upper=1
    )
    conversion_rate_treatment = pm.Uniform(
        "conversion_rate_treatment", lower=0, upper=1
    )
    relative_lift = pm.Deterministic(
        "relative_lift",
        conversion_rate_treatment / conversion_rate_control - 1,
    )

\[\begin{align*} \text{cr}_\text{c} & \sim \text{Beta}(1, 1) \\ \text{cr}_\text{t} & \sim \text{Beta}(1, 1) \\ \text{lift} & = \text{cr}_\text{t} / \text{cr}_\text{c} - 1 \end{align*}\]

Prior Predictive Modeling in Bayesian AB Testing

Independent Priors

Independent priors give implausible lifts.

The relative lift is a ratio of two random variables. Flat priors on the two conversion rates allow a treatment that is \(100\) times better than control. Informative priors that put both rates near \(2.4\%\) still allow lifts of \(50\%\), which are rare in practice.

Flat: \(\text{cr}_\text{c}, \text{cr}_\text{t} \sim \text{Beta}(1, 1)\)

Informative: \(\text{cr}_\text{c}, \text{cr}_\text{t} \sim \text{Beta}(15, 600)\)

Prior Predictive Modeling in Bayesian AB Testing

Correlated Priors

Most true lifts are small. So we set the prior on the relative lift itself, and the joint prior of the two rates concentrates on the diagonal.

with pm.Model() as correlated_model:
    conversion_rate_control = pm.Beta(
        "conversion_rate_control", alpha=15, beta=600
    )
    relative_lift = pm.Normal(
        "relative_lift", mu=0, sigma=0.1
    )
    conversion_rate_treatment = pm.Deterministic(
        "conversion_rate_treatment",
        conversion_rate_control * (1 + relative_lift),
    )

Prior Predictive Modeling in Bayesian AB Testing

Inference with Correlated Priors

We observe \(200\) and \(350\) conversions out of \(10{,}000\) users per group (naive lift \(75\%\)).

with correlated_model:
    pm.Binomial("n_control", n=n, p=conversion_rate_control, observed=n_c)
    pm.Binomial("n_treatment", n=n, p=conversion_rate_treatment, observed=n_t)

The posterior lift is \(31\%\) (\(94\%\) HDI \([18\%, 43\%]\)), and both observed rates fall outside their HDIs. Twyman’s law says that any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong, so we check the experiment before we trust it.

Observed Confounders

Without randomization, we must adjust for confounders. We use the LaLonde data from a job training program (National Supported Work Demonstration, 1970s) and ask whether job training increases earnings.

  • Treatment: participation in the job training program (treat).
  • Outcome: earnings in 1978 (re78, in thousands of USD).
  • Covariates: education, age, earnings in 1975 (re75), race and ethnicity, marital status, degree.
  • Naive comparison: the treated group earned \(635\) USD less than the comparison group. The \(185\) treated units come from the experiment, but the \(429\) comparison units come from an observational survey, so treatment was not randomly assigned.

We assume that the covariates block every backdoor path (the backdoor criterion).

Introduction to Causal Inference with PPLs

Regression and the do Operator

We have two ways to estimate the ATE:

  1. We fit a linear regression and read the estimate of \(\beta_{\text{treat}}\) as the ATE:

    \[ \text{earnings} = \beta_0 + \beta_{\text{treat}} \cdot \text{treat} + \beta_{\text{covariates}}^{\top} \text{covariates} + \varepsilon \]

  2. We use the do operator:

    \[ \text{ATE} = \text{E}[\text{earnings} \mid \text{do}(\text{treat} = 1)] - \text{E}[\text{earnings} \mid \text{do}(\text{treat} = 0)] \]

Pearl’s do Operator

\(\text{P}(Y \mid \text{do}(T = t))\) is the distribution of \(Y\) when we set \(T = t\) for every unit. In the graph, it cuts all arrows into \(T\), as in a randomized experiment.

Introduction to Causal Inference with PPLs

The PyMC Model

We write both parts of the DAG as code: covariates \(\to\) treat, and treat plus covariates \(\to\) earnings.

with pm.Model(coords=coords) as earnings_model:
    covariates = pm.Data("covariates_data", covariates_obs, dims=("obs_idx", "covariate"))
    # covariates -> treat
    intercept_treat = pm.Normal("intercept_treat", mu=0, sigma=10)
    beta_covariate_treat = pm.Normal("beta_covariate_treat", mu=0, sigma=1, dims="covariate")
    treat = pm.Bernoulli(
        "treat", logit_p=intercept_treat + covariates @ beta_covariate_treat, dims="obs_idx"
    )
    # treat and covariates -> earnings
    intercept_earnings = pm.Normal("intercept_earnings", mu=0, sigma=10)
    beta_treat_earnings = pm.Normal("beta_treat_earnings", mu=0, sigma=1)
    beta_covariate_earnings = pm.Normal("beta_covariate_earnings", mu=0, sigma=1, dims="covariate")
    sigma_earnings = pm.HalfNormal("sigma_earnings", sigma=10)
    mu_earnings = pm.Deterministic(
        "mu_earnings",
        intercept_earnings + beta_treat_earnings * treat + covariates @ beta_covariate_earnings,
        dims="obs_idx",
    )
    pm.Normal("earnings", mu=mu_earnings, sigma=sigma_earnings, dims="obs_idx")

Conditioning and Intervening

observe conditions the generative model on the data, so the same program now defines the likelihood. do sets treat to \(0\) or \(1\) for every unit and removes the arrows from the covariates into treat.

from pymc.model.transform.conditioning import do, observe

conditioned_model = observe(
    earnings_model, {"treat": training_obs, "earnings": earnings_obs}
)
do_0_model = do(conditioned_model, {"treat": np.zeros(n_obs, dtype=np.int32)})
do_1_model = do(conditioned_model, {"treat": np.ones(n_obs, dtype=np.int32)})

In NumPyro, the numpyro.handlers.do effect handler does the same.

A Gamma GLM

  • Earnings are non-negative and right-skewed. \(23\%\) are exactly zero (we shift them by a small constant).
  • We change two lines: a softplus link keeps the mean positive, and pm.Gamma replaces pm.Normal.
  • With a non-linear link, the coefficient is not the ATE.

The same do code works with a non-linear link. A hurdle model for the zeros is one possible extension.

Hierarchies and Latent Confounders

We simulate \(2{,}000\) individuals in \(30\) groups. An unobserved group effect \(G\) drives both \(X\) and the binary outcome \(Y\), and the true effect of \(X\) on \(Y\) is zero.

  • The naive model (\(0.68\), \(94\%\) HDI \([0.61, 0.76]\)) and the multilevel model with random intercepts (\(0.21\), \([0.07, 0.38]\)) are biased.
  • Fixed effects (\(0.03\)), the Mundlak model that adds the group mean of \(X\) (\(0.03\)), and a latent group variable \(u_g\) with its own likelihood for \(X\) (\(0.04\), best by LOO) all cover zero.

Latent Confounders in NumPyro

We estimate the effect of side-quest engagement on in-game purchases in an online game. We treat the confounders we leave out as one unobserved confounder \(Z\).

\(Z \sim \text{Normal}(0, 1)\) per player. Neural networks link \(Z\) to engagement and purchases, and an encoder approximates its posterior (CEVAE, Louizos et al., 2017). We fit the model with variational inference. The predictive fit is skewed, so the model needs more work.

We sample \(Z\) once and intervene on engagement with numpyro.handlers.do. The ATE has mean \(109\) (\(94\%\) HDI \([-46, 265]\)), close to the DoWhy frontdoor estimate of \(170\).

Marketing Mix Models

In marketing, budgets are rarely randomized, so we estimate channel effects from observational time series.

How should we split next quarter’s marketing budget across channels (TV, radio, search, and so on) to maximize sales?

A marketing mix model (MMM) is a regression of sales \(y_t\) on transformed media spend \(x_{m,t}\) and control variables \(z_{k,t}\) (trend, seasonality, holidays):

\[ y_t = \beta_0 + \sum_{m} \beta_m f_m(x_{m,t}) + \sum_{k} \gamma_k z_{k,t} + \varepsilon_t \]

where \(f_m\) is the media transformation of channel \(m\) (next slide).

The data are short (typically \(2\) to \(3\) years of weekly data), the media channels are correlated, and some confounders are unobserved. Bayesian MMMs add domain knowledge through priors, calibration with experiments, and posterior uncertainty for budget decisions.

Jin, Yuxue, et al. “Bayesian methods for media mix modeling with carryover and shape effects.” (2017).

Adstock and Saturation

The adstock transformation models carryover: the effect of an ad persists and decays over time.

\[ \text{adstock}(x_{m, t}; \alpha, L) = \frac{\sum_{j=0}^{L-1} \alpha^{j} x_{m, t-j}}{\sum_{j=0}^{L-1} \alpha^{j}} \]

for \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) and \(L\) the maximum lag.

The saturation transformation models diminishing returns: each extra unit of spend has a smaller effect.

\[ \text{saturation}(x; \eta) = \frac{1 - \exp(-\eta x)}{1 + \exp(-\eta x)} \]

for \(\eta > 0\).

Adstock: Jin et al. (2017). Saturation: PyMC-Marketing LogisticSaturation.

Return on Ad Spend

An MMM splits sales into a baseline, seasonality, and channel contributions (right).

The return on ad spend (ROAS) of channel \(m\) compares the expected sales with and without the channel, per unit of spend. It is an ATE per unit of spend, for a continuous treatment that was not randomized.

\[ \text{ROAS}_m = \frac{\sum_t \left(\text{E}[y_t \mid \text{do}(x_m = x_m^{\text{obs}})] - \text{E}[y_t \mid \text{do}(x_m = 0)]\right)}{\sum_t x_{m,t}} \]

Unobserved Confounders

We simulate \(2.5\) years of weekly data with two channels, x1 and x2, and sales y.

  • z is a confounder: it drives the spend on x1 and the sales y.
  • The models include trend and seasonality. If we leave out z, the backdoor path through z stays open.
  • Model 1 includes z. Model 2 leaves z out, as if we could not observe it.
  • We know the true ROAS of each channel because we simulate the data.

This is the job training example again, but we cannot adjust for z, and the data offer no proxies to model it as a latent variable.

Biased ROAS

A model without z estimates a ROAS of \(198\) for x1 against a true value of \(93\) (orange line), and \(142\) for x2 against \(171\). So the model ranks x1 above x2, while the true ranking is the reverse.

We bring experimental results into the model in one of two ways: priors on the ROAS (Zhang et al., 2024) or an additional likelihood for lift tests, which are experiments on one channel (PyMC-Marketing).

Calibration with Experiments

We use the same idea as the correlated priors in the A/B test: we put the prior on the quantity we know something about, here the ROAS.

We write each media coefficient as a function of the channel’s ROAS (simplified):

\[ \beta_m = \frac{\text{ROAS}_m \sum_{t} x_{m,t}}{\sum_{t} \left(f(x_{m,t}) - f(\tilde{x}_{m,t})\right)} \]

where \(f\) is the media transformation (adstock and saturation) and \(\tilde{x}_{m}\) is the spend with channel \(m\) turned off.

With these priors and still without z, the ROAS of x1 moves from about \(200\) to about \(120\) (true value \(93\)).

Now we can put a prior on the ROAS of each channel, for example from a past experiment. In the simulation, we center the priors at \(95\) and \(170\), close to the truth (shown below on the scaled data).

Zhang et al. (2024) and Media Mix Model and Experimental Calibration: A Simulation Study

Lift Test Calibration

A lift test is an A/B test on one channel. We change the spend in some regions or time periods and measure the change in sales. Here the lift is an absolute change in sales, not a relative one.

  • Each test measures how much the saturation curve rises from \(x\) to \(x + \Delta x\).
  • The model predicts this lift from the curve. The measured lift enters as an additional likelihood.
  • The saturation parameters must explain the sales and the experiments.
  • Each new test adds one more observation.

PyMC-Marketing v0.10.0: Lift Test Calibration

Lift Test Results

With lift tests and still without z, the ROAS of x1 moves from \(198\) to \(109\) (\(94\%\) HDI \([98, 121]\), true value \(93\), orange line). For x2, we get \(161\) (\(94\%\) HDI \([143, 179]\), true value \(171\)). The bias on x1 drops from \(+112\%\) to \(+17\%\), and the ranking of the two channels is now correct.

Funnel Models in Marketing

An omitted confounder biased the previous models. Funnels add a different risk: a mediator that we add as a regressor. We use the PyMC-Marketing MMM case study (\(209\) weeks of synthetic sales data).

  • Upper funnel channels build awareness: TV, Radio, Newspaper.
  • Lower funnel channels capture existing demand, like paid search, also called search engine marketing (SEM): sem_spend.
  • Paid search is \(26.9\%\) of the modeled media spend.
  • Its spend and impressions (how often the ads show) both correlate with sales (\(0.59\)).

Two stories fit the data: paid search drives sales, or it harvests demand that the upper funnel created. Correlation cannot separate them.

PyMC-Marketing v1.2.0: MMM Case Study II

The Funnel DAG

  • TV, Radio, and Newspaper act on sales directly and through latent demand \(D\).
  • The paid search budget \(B\) adds to \(D\). The sum is the mediator \(M^*\) (the demand that paid search reaches). Here \(B\) is exogenous.
  • Search impressions measure \(M^*\) and do not cause sales. Dashed nodes are latent.
  • In practice, paid search spend also follows demand, so adding it as a channel conditions on the mediator (Chan and Perry, 2017: “Downstream ads should not be included with exogenously-determined ads in a single regression equation.”).

PyMC-Marketing v1.2.0: MMM Case Study II

Funnel Model Structure

Let \(U_{t,c}\) be the spend of upper funnel channel \(c\) in week \(t\), \(B_t\) the paid search spend, and \(I_t\) the search impressions (\(uf\) and \(lf\) mark the upper and lower funnel):

\[ \begin{align*} D_t & = \text{baseline} + \sum_{c \in \text{upper}} \text{sat}^{uf}\left(\text{adstock}^{uf}(U_{t,c})\right) \\ M^{*}_t & = D_t + \lambda \, \frac{B_t}{s_{B}} \\ I_t & \sim \text{TruncatedNormal}\left(s_{I} \, M^{*}_t, \sigma_{I}, \text{lower}=0\right) \end{align*} \]

Sales get the extra term \(\text{sat}^{lf}\left(\text{adstock}^{lf}(M^{*}_t)\right)\). Here \(\lambda\) converts paid search spend into search demand, \(s_{B}\) and \(s_{I}\) are the series maxima, and \(\sigma_I\) is the measurement noise.

A Structural Equation Model

This is a structural equation model with a latent variable. The impressions equation is the measurement model. We fix its loading at one (on \(I_t / s_I\)) to set the scale of \(M^*\).

PyMC-Marketing v1.2.0: MMM Case Study II

Direct and Mediated Contributions

  • We compute the mediated part with an intervention: we set the spend of one upper funnel channel to zero and push the change through \(D\) and \(M^*\) to sales.
  • The mediated share of each channel’s total effect is about \(6\%\) (Newspaper), \(12\%\) (TV), and \(25\%\) (Radio). The lower ends of the \(94\%\) HDIs reach zero.
  • The shares are small because about \(41\%\) of the mediator comes from the baseline and \(49\%\) from paid search’s own spend. Only about \(10\%\) comes from upper funnel media.

Causal Identification in Funnel Models

We make the funnel more realistic: paid search spend responds to demand, there are three geos (regions), and unobserved noise is shared across media plans. We simulate \(130\) weeks from the funnel model with the true parameters fixed via pm.do, so we know the truth.

  • Mediator: in the case study, paid search spend was the input \(B\). Here lower_spend (\(M\)) measures the latent \(M^* = D + \lambda B\), and \(B\) is a separate budget lf_budget.
  • Indicator: branded search search_volume (\(S\), searches for the brand name) measures latent demand \(D\). It does not cause sales.
  • Confounding: tv_spend, social_spend, and category demand (demand for the product category) share seasonality, trend, and unobserved noise.

The Causal DAG

  • Mediator vs indicator: we ask whether doubling the variable would move sales. For lower funnel spend it would, so it is a mediator. For branded search it would not, so it is an indicator.
  • Exclusion restriction: the budget \(B\) enters \(M^*\) but not \(S\). This identifies \(\lambda\), and nothing else in the model can replace it. That category demand \(C\) enters \(D\) but not \(Y\) is a modeling convenience, because \(C\) is observed.
  • The driver noise \(N\) is unobserved. It confounds tv_spend, social_spend, and category demand.

Testable and Untestable Assumptions

Untestable

The funnel model is correct only if the demand equation is right and nothing unobserved confounds \(M^* \to Y\). The backdoor checks cannot verify these assumptions. Posterior predictive checks of lower_spend and search_volume can reveal some errors in the demand equation, but not an unobserved confounder of \(M^* \to Y\).

Testable

The DAG implies that the budget \(B\) and branded search \(S\) are independent (\(B \perp S\)), but not given \(Y\) or \(M\). In the data, the correlations per geo are \(-0.070\), \(+0.046\), and \(-0.101\), against a standard error of about \(0.09\). The data does not contradict the DAG.

A Fair Comparison

Here, the funnel model is the true data-generating process. It also fits two extra series (lower_spend, search_volume) and uses two extra inputs (category_demand, lf_budget). The gain comes from structure and information together.

Predictive Fit

  • The in-sample \(R^2\) of the six models ranges from \(0.979\) to \(0.987\), while their ROAS estimates differ by tens of percent.
  • tv_spend: category demand enters B+ as a linear control, while the media effects are flexible curves, so the curves absorb part of the demand effect. The invalid set B++ has a mean error of \(4.4\%\), and the valid set B+ has \(15.3\%\).
  • social_spend: every model overstates it in south, including the funnel model at \(+27\%\). B+ is \(100\%\) too high there and \(40\%\) too high in west. The backdoor is open on the graph but at noise level in the data, so the control removes little. A separate response curve per channel cannot represent the shared, saturating mediated pool.

Takeaways

Causal Inference with PPLs

  • The model states the assumptions. We write the DAG as code: job training, MMM, and funnel models.
  • Priors and experiments carry outside information. Priors rule out implausible lifts in A/B tests. ROAS priors and lift test likelihoods reduce confounding bias in MMMs (on x1, from \(+112\%\) to \(+17\%\)).
  • The same do query works across models. It applies to a linear regression, a Gamma GLM, a latent variable model in NumPyro, and a funnel model with latent demand.
  • Hierarchies and latent variables extend the model. A latent group variable removes group-level confounding, and measurement equations link impressions and branded search to latent demand.
  • Identification comes first. The DAG tells us which variables we need. The functional form still matters, and a good in-sample fit replaces neither.
